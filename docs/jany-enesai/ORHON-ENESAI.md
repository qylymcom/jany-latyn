## Abstract

Orhon-Enesaí is a proposed output mode for the Jany-Latyn testbed. It writes modern Kyrgyz in the Old Turkic script, the runes of the Orkhon and Yenisei inscriptions, which Unicode has encoded since 2009. It keeps the constraint that governs Jany-Latyn: every runic spelling is computed from Cyrillic by rule, without a word list, so that any Kyrgyz text can be rendered unattended. The rune stage reads the canonical Jany-Latyn full form, not Cyrillic, so it inherits every decision that orthography has already made about `е`, `ё`, `ю`, `я`, `ъ`, `ь`, `ц`, and `щ`. It adds one rule of its own: each consonant takes the back or front form that agrees with the vowel of its syllable. That split is the defining structure of the script. Unlike the `q`/`ğ` split Jany-Latyn declines, it is derivable, because it records agreement with a vowel that Cyrillic writes rather than a pronunciation that only a lexicon knows. The letter assignments follow a Kyrgyz chart circulating as a short video, and the converter reproduces that chart's own three-word title exactly. Two kinds of departure from Unicode are stated rather than hidden. The chart presses Orkhon and Yenisei variants into service to tell `ы` from `и` and `ө` from `ү`, and it uses signs that Unicode names as other letters for `ж`, `ф`, `х`, and `в`; a Unicode-strict preset is offered beside it. One loss cannot be engineered away: the script has a single sign for `о` and `у`. Conversion back to Cyrillic is therefore exact for every word without those vowels and has to guess within the rest. On held-out text from a Kyrgyz treebank, the guess leaves 88% of all word tokens exact, even though only 0.4% of word types are true homographs.

## About this document

This is a design proposal for one more mode of an existing testbed. It is not a standard, not a reconstruction of how the Yenisei Kyrgyz wrote, and not an argument that Kyrgyz should be written in runes. Like `WHITEPAPER.md`, it argues for a set of choices, states the reasoning behind each, and names the places where the reasoning could be wrong.

Unlike `WHITEPAPER.md`, it has some numbers. A reference prototype of the converter was written for this draft and run over the 2,520 sentences of the UD Kyrgyz-KTMU treebank [[C1]](#refC1). Every figure that comes from that run is marked with the corpus it was measured on. Each runic example in this document was generated by the prototype, not written by hand. Statements about how readers would find the result are predictions, and §20 lists them with the tests that would settle them.

The sources disagree with one another in places: a community chart, the Unicode encoding, and the historical inscriptions. Where they do, this document says which one decides and why.

# Part I — Orhon-Enesaí in Brief

Orhon-Enesaí writes any modern Kyrgyz text in the runes of the Orkhon and Yenisei inscriptions, computed from Cyrillic with no one checking the result. *Кыргыз* becomes 𐰶𐰃𐰺𐰍𐰃𐰕, read from right to left: the back-series `к` that belongs beside `ы`, then `ы`, `р`, the back-series `г`, `ы` again, and `з`. Each consonant agrees with the vowel of its syllable, which is how the script was built. *Көл* (lake) takes the front-series signs and becomes 𐰜𐰇𐰠.

The name is the Kyrgyz *Орхон-Энесай* written in Jany-Latyn: *Orhon-Enesaí* in the full form, *Orhon-Enesai* in the plain form. The plain form is also the mode's identifier in the software.

## 1. What the Mode Is For

The mode serves four uses. It renders modern text in the script for display, design, and signage. It helps a Cyrillic-literate reader learn to read the inscriptions' letters. It gives heritage and educational material one consistent convention where there is currently a scatter of hand-made charts. And, as with every other setting in the testbed, it lets the conventions themselves be compared on real text instead of on a handful of words.

The mode is not a reconstruction. The Yenisei inscriptions were written in a language a millennium older than modern Kyrgyz, with conventions this mode does not follow, chiefly the omission of most vowels (§8). It is also not a claim about how Old Kyrgyz sounded, and in particular it takes no side on whether the inscriptions record the `ж` of modern Kyrgyz (§8). What it offers is narrower: a documented, reproducible way to put modern Kyrgyz into these letters, with every place where that requires a choice made visible and switchable.

| **Wikipedia**, "Old Turkic script" [[W1]](#refW1) | Transliteration values and the back/front pairing of the consonants, as a cross-check on the codepoint matches |
| **Jany-Latyn**, `WHITEPAPER.md` [[JL]](#refJL) | The principles, the pivot form, and every decision about loanword letters |

The chart is a community artifact of unknown authorship. Its Arabic labels are in the Arabic-based Kyrgyz orthography used in Xinjiang, which writes ы, и, ө, and ү with distinct letters (ى, ئ, ۅ, ۉ), so its vowel distinctions can be read off without guessing. It decides the letter assignments for a practical reason. It is what Kyrgyz readers are already being shown, and a converter that silently disagreed with it would teach a second convention instead of documenting the first. Where it conflicts with Unicode, the conflict is set out in §7, and the Unicode-strict preset resolves it the other way.

### 2.2 The Unicode block in brief

The block runs from U+10C00 to U+10C4F and has 73 assigned characters, U+10C00 to U+10C48, added in Unicode 5.2 in 2009 [[W1]](#refW1). Every character is a letter (general category `Lo`) with right-to-left directionality (`Bidi_Class` R), in the script `Old_Turkic`, ISO 15924 `Orkh` (175). The character names describe the sign, not the language it writes: `ORKHON AQ` is the back-series `q` of the Orkhon tradition, `YENISEI AEY` the front-series `y` of the Yenisei one. The proposal notes that these names "do not imply more than" the typical area of use, and that the Orkhon and Yenisei forms were encoded as separate characters because "the distribution of the forms cannot simply be predicted by the provenance of an inscription" [[U3]](#refU3). That decision is what makes the chart's vowel distinctions possible in plain text (§7.1).

### 2.3 How the reading and the figures were produced

The video is 9.75 seconds long and shows only the two charts. Frames were extracted, and each of the 50 drawn signs was matched against the glyphs of Noto Sans Old Turkic [[F1]](#refF1), with the Wikipedia transliterations as a cross-check. Appendix A gives the result sign by sign. Three readings are less certain than the rest, and they are flagged where they matter: row 5 (`λ`, read as U+10C33), row 43 (an angle with a long right stroke, read as U+10C0A rather than the plain angle U+10C40), and row 48 (a single zigzag, read as U+10C29).

The figures come from a TypeScript prototype of the rule in §14, given to the implementation with this proposal, and run over UD Kyrgyz-KTMU [[C1]](#refC1): 2,520 sentences of news and fiction, 19,587 Cyrillic word tokens, and 6,269 word types. The prototype's own Cyrillic-to-Jany-Latyn front end reproduces the example sentences of `WHITEPAPER.md` §2 and §21.2 character for character, which is the check that it feeds the rune stage what the real engine would.

Four signs are bound to a particular vowel as well as a series: the `к` by `о`/`у` (𐰸), by `ө`/`ү` (𐰜), and by `ы` (𐰶), and the `т` by `о`/`у` (𐱇). They are what the chart lists as syllables (قو, كو, قى, تو), and the vowel is written after them too (§6.1). The Unicode names in the tables show where the chart departs from Unicode's own reading of a sign. §7 takes those departures up.

`ц` and `щ` do not appear. Jany-Latyn has already written them `ts` and `ş`, and the rune stage never sees them (§4).

## 4. The Rule: One Pivot, One Governor

Conversion runs in two stages:

1. **Cyrillic → Jany-Latyn full form.** This is the existing engine with every testbed option at its Jany-Latyn default, whatever the reader has selected for display.
2. **Jany-Latyn full form → runes.** Each letter is mapped by the tables in §3. A vowel maps directly. A consonant takes its form from its *governing vowel*, the vowel of its syllable.

The governing vowel is found the way a Kyrgyz word is syllabified. A medial syllable has exactly one consonant before its vowel (V.CV, VC.CV, VCC.CV), so a consonant directly before a vowel governs from that vowel, and any other consonant governs from the vowel before it. Consonants at the start of a word, including the clusters of loans such as *спорт*, take the first vowel. Consonants at the end take the last. A word with no vowel at all, such as an abbreviation, takes the back series.

Going through Jany-Latyn rather than directly from Cyrillic is the coherence argument in its simplest form. Every decision Jany-Latyn makes about the loan layer carries over unchanged: the iotated `е` of *Европа* is already `íe`, the soft sign of *семья* is already absorbed into `í`, and `ц` is already `ts`. So *Европа* is 𐰘𐰅𐰊𐰺𐰆𐰯𐰀 and *семья* is 𐰾𐰅𐰢𐰖𐰀, with no second set of rules to keep in step with the first. The canonical input also isolates the runes from the testbed's Latin toggles: a reader viewing `q` mode or the dotless `ı` sees the same runes as one viewing Jany-Latyn, because the rune stage never reads the display form.

The governor is a property of the syllable rather than the word, and that matters for about a fifth of the vocabulary. In the corpus, 21.0% of word types contain both back and front vowels: loans such as *компьютер*, compounds such as *темиржол* (railway), and the suffixed forms of both. *Темиржол* comes out as 𐱅𐰅𐰢𐰄𐰼𐰳𐰆𐰞, front signs in *темир* and back signs in *жол*, which is what a writer agreeing each consonant with its vowel would produce.

# Part II — Congruity: The Sources Against Each Other

## 6. With the Chart

### 6.1 The chart's own title

The best evidence of how the chart's author meant the signs to be used is the one place where the author used them: the title line in runes beneath the Arabic. All three words come out of the converter exactly as the chart writes them (runes read right to left):

| Title word | The chart's rune line | The converter |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Байыркы | 𐰉𐰀𐰖𐰃𐰺𐰶𐰃 | 𐰉𐰀𐰖𐰃𐰺𐰶𐰃 |
| кыргыз | 𐰶𐰃𐰺𐰍𐰃𐰕 | 𐰶𐰃𐰺𐰍𐰃𐰕 |
| алфавити | 𐰀𐰞𐰟𐰀𐰊𐰄𐱅𐰄 | 𐰀𐰞𐰟𐰀𐰊𐰄𐱅𐰄 |

The match settles four points the table of signs alone would leave open:

- **Every vowel is written.** The title writes the `а` and every `ы`, which the inscriptions usually would not (§8). This is the *plene* register of §12, and it is the default.
- **The syllabic signs are used, and the vowel is written after them anyway.** *Кыргыз* begins 𐰶𐰃, the `к`-by-`ы` sign followed by `ы`, not 𐰶 alone.
- **Harmony is decided per syllable.** *Алфавити* has the back `л` 𐰞 in its first syllable and the front `т` 𐱅 in its last.
- **The runes follow the Cyrillic spelling, not the Arabic one.** The Arabic title above them writes الفاۋىتى, with `ы` (ى) in its last two syllables. The rune line writes `и` (𐰄) and the front `т`, as in Cyrillic *алфавити*. The chart's author was, in effect, already converting from Cyrillic.

### 6.2 What the chart leaves open, and what this proposal decides

| Point | The chart | This proposal |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Two signs labelled ا (rows 1, 41) | 𐰀 and 𐰁 both given for `а` | 𐰀 is written; 𐰁 stays reachable on the keyboard but is never emitted |
| Row 2, labelled ə | 𐰂, a front low vowel Kyrgyz Cyrillic does not write | Emitted only for the compose-mode `ä` of `WHITEPAPER.md` §20, so a person writing southern *äkä* in Latin gets it in runes, with front-series consonants beside it |
| Row 8, 𐰱 labelled چئ (чи) | Given for `ч` with `и` | Used when `ч`'s governing vowel is `и`; beside `ы`, `ч` is 𐰲 |
| Rows 21–23, 40: 𐰸 𐰜 𐰶 𐱇 | Listed as syllables (قو, كو, قى, تو) | Used whenever `к` or `т` governs from that vowel, and the vowel is written too (§6.1) |
| Long vowels | Not shown | Doubled, as in Cyrillic and Jany-Latyn: *тоо* 𐱇𐰆𐰆 |
| Word divider | The title uses spaces | Spaces by default; U+205A ⁚ as an option (§18) |
| Footer: 𐰡 (лт), 𐰧 (нт), 𐰩 (нч) | Listed as "double consonants" | Used only in the epigraphic register (§15), where the inscriptions' practice is the point |
| Footer: 𐰪 labelled ڭ | A second sign for `ң`, which row 29 already gives as 𐰭 | Not emitted. Unicode names it `ORKHON ENY`, the palatal nasal `ny` |

## 7. With Unicode

### 7.1 What Unicode allows, and what it assumes

Unicode encodes the Orkhon and Yenisei forms of a letter as different characters, but it does not give them different values. 𐰃 `ORKHON I` and 𐰄 `YENISEI I` are both the letter `i`/`ı`, and a text using one rather than the other records a tradition, not a vowel. The chart turns three such pairs into phonemic distinctions, and it gives five signs a value their Unicode names do not have:

| Kyrgyz | Chart sign | Unicode name | Unicode's own value | Kind of departure |
| :--- | :---: | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| ы / и | 𐰃 / 𐰄 | `ORKHON I` / `YENISEI I` | Orkhon and Yenisei forms of one `i`/`ı` | Variant pressed into service |
| ө / ү | 𐰇 / 𐰈 | `ORKHON OE` / `YENISEI OE` | Orkhon and Yenisei forms of one `ö`/`ü` | Variant pressed into service |
| ж (back) | 𐰳 | `YENISEI EC` | `č`, the Yenisei form of 𐰲 | Repurposed |
| ж (front) | 𐰙 | `YENISEI AEY` | Front `y`, the Yenisei form of 𐰘 | Repurposed, though etymologically apt (§7.3) |
| ф | 𐰟 | `YENISEI AL` | Back `l`, the Yenisei form of 𐰞 | Repurposed |
| х | 𐰝 | `YENISEI OEK` | Front `k` by `ö`/`ü`, the Yenisei form of 𐰜 | Repurposed |
| в | 𐰊 | `YENISEI AB` | Back `b`, the Yenisei form of 𐰉 | Repurposed, mildly: the sign is a form of `b`, the nearest native consonant |

That Unicode encoded the variants separately is what makes the chart workable in plain text at all. Had the Orkhon and Yenisei `I` been one character with two glyphs, no file could tell *кыз* from *киз* by its vowel. They are separate characters, so the distinction survives copying, search, and any font.

The cost is in what software assumes. Default collation, which is what a database, a spreadsheet, or a browser's `Intl.Collator` uses unless told otherwise, treats each Orkhon/Yenisei pair as one letter. Checked under ICU 77.1 (Unicode 16) at base strength, every pair in the table compares equal to the other member of its pair: 𐰄 to 𐰃, 𐰈 to 𐰇, 𐰳 to 𐰲, 𐰙 to 𐰘, 𐰟 to 𐰞, 𐰝 to 𐰜, and 𐰊 to 𐰉. The back and front series of one letter, such as 𐰉 and 𐰋, stay distinct. In practice this means:

- **Rendering:** no effect. Every font with the block draws every character.
- **Sorting:** the default order files `ы` with `и`, `ж` with `ч`, and `ф` with `л`. The testbed needs its own comparison, as Jany-Latyn already does, because `Intl.Collator` accepts no tailoring (`WHITEPAPER.md` Appendix C, and §13 here).
- **Search:** a matcher that ignores accents and case at base strength would find `ч` when asked for `ж`, and `л` when asked for `ф`. Whether browsers' find-in-page behaves this way for Old Turkic is a prediction, not checked.
- **Interoperability:** any tool that reads the characters by their Unicode values, such as a scholarly transliterator, would read 𐰳𐰆𐰞 (*жол*) as *čol* and 𐰟𐰀𐰉𐰼𐰄𐰴𐰀 (*фабрика*) as *labrika* with a Yenisei `l`.

### 7.2 How much text it touches

In the corpus, `ж` occurs in 10.3% of word tokens, `в` in 2.7%, `ф` in 1.3%, and `х` in 0.3%: 14.3% of tokens contain at least one repurposed sign. The pressed-into-service vowel variants are in most words, since `ы` and `и` are the commonest vowels of the language.

### 7.3 The Unicode-strict preset

The Unicode-strict preset keeps the vowel distinctions, since they use characters for values within their names, and replaces the five repurposings:

- **`ж` as the y-runes**, back 𐰖 and front 𐰘. This is not an improvisation. Kyrgyz word-initial `ж` corresponds regularly to the `y` of Old Turkic and most other Turkic languages (Old Turkic *yol*, *yer*; Kyrgyz *жол*, *жер*) [[JL26]](#refJL), so the strict preset writes 𐰖𐰆𐰞 for *жол*, the spelling of the cognate in the inscriptions. It merges `ж` with `й`, but the two rarely contrast in the same position, since `ж` begins syllables and `й` mostly ends them. In the corpus the merger creates no new homograph: the strict preset produces the same 12 shared spellings as the default (§11), all of them `о`/`у` pairs.
- **`ф` as `п`, `х` as the `к` series, `в` as the `б` series**, the nearest native consonant in each case. For `х` it is also the substitution spoken Kyrgyz itself made in older loans, as in *Ыскак* from *Исхак* (`WHITEPAPER.md` §20). *Фабрика* becomes 𐰯𐰀𐰉𐰼𐰄𐰴𐰀 and *рахмат* 𐰺𐰀𐰴𐰢𐰀𐱄.

Which of the two should be the testbed's default is a decision for the maintainer. This draft defaults to the chart, because the chart is what readers are being shown, and records the question in §20.6.

## 8. With the Inscriptions

The plene register departs from the inscriptions on purpose, and the departures are listed here so that no reader mistakes the output for a reconstruction:

- **Vowels.** The inscriptions write few vowels: "initial and medial vowels are often not explicitly written, though in final syllables they often are" [[U3]](#refU3). The consonant series carries the harmony the vowel would have. The epigraphic register (§15) imitates that tendency, and the inscription preset combines it with the historical vowel letters. The default does neither, because a reader of modern Kyrgyz needs the vowels, and because a spelling without them cannot be converted back.
- **One vowel letter per pair.** The inscriptions use one sign for `ı`/`i`, one for `o`/`u`, and one for `ö`/`ü`, with the consonants telling the members apart. The chart's distinctions for `ы`/`и` and `ө`/`ү` are a modern addition. They cannot be extended to `о`/`у`, because Unicode has only one `O` (§11).
- **`ж`.** Whether the Yenisei inscriptions already record the Kyrgyz shift of `y-` to `ж-` is a question for Turkologists that this document does not try to settle. The chart gives `ж` signs of its own; the strict preset writes the cognate `y`.
- **Direction and lines.** The inscriptions run right to left in rows read from bottom to top, while modern editions keep the right-to-left direction and set the rows top to bottom [[U3]](#refU3). The testbed follows the modern editions.

# Part III — Coherence With Jany-Latyn

## 9. Principle by Principle

| Jany-Latyn principle (`WHITEPAPER.md` §3) | How Orhon-Enesaí meets it | What it costs |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Cyrillic-derivability** | No word list. One rule beyond Jany-Latyn's own: the governing vowel (§4) | Nothing in the forward direction; the reverse loses `о`/`у` (§11) |
| **2. Input ergonomics** | A layout on the Kyrgyz Cyrillic positions. Runes have no case, so Shift gives the front series, and every rune the plene presets emit is on the base or Shift layer (§19) | Typing runes directly means choosing back or front for each consonant; the playground's live conversion makes that unnecessary |
| **3. Phonological economy** | No sign is added beyond the chart | The script itself spends two signs on each of ten consonants. That is its structure, not this mode's addition |
| **4. Typographic and computational stability** | One script inside every word; standard codepoints; no case at all, so the casing failures of §9 there cannot occur; NFC and NFD are identical; every word matches `\p{L}+` | Characters outside the Basic Multilingual Plane (§9.1); right-to-left layout (§18); thin system font support (§18); default collation merges the chart's variant pairs (§7.1) |
| **5. Two registers by design** | Plene and epigraphic, specified together (§12) | The epigraphic register is one-way, as the plain form is |
| **Reverse conversion and the native-priority rule** (§18 there) | Every word without `о` or `у` restores exactly | `о`/`у` is a loss in native vocabulary, where the native-priority rule has nothing to choose between (§11) |

### 9.1 The cost of the supplementary plane

Every rune is outside the Basic Multilingual Plane, so it takes two UTF-16 code units and four UTF-8 bytes. *Кыргыз* is six letters in either script. In Cyrillic it is twelve UTF-8 bytes; in runes it is twenty-four, and its JavaScript `length` is 12, not 6. Nothing in the engine may index, slice, or count a rune string by code unit. Iteration has to be by code point, with `for…of`, `Array.from`, or the `u` flag on every regular expression. This is the commonest way a correct converter produces broken output, so the implementation brief makes it a test.

## 10. The Split That Is Derivable

Jany-Latyn declines `q` and `ğ` because they are *splits*: one Cyrillic letter becomes two Latin letters depending on the word, and Kyrgyz Cyrillic does not record which words are borrowed (`WHITEPAPER.md` §3, §8). A rule that writes *кар* as *qar* also writes *космос* as *qosmos*. The runic script splits ten consonants into a back form and a front form, and this mode applies the split to every word with no list at all. The two positions are consistent, because the two splits are conditioned on different things.

A Latin `q` asserts a pronunciation, the uvular [q]. The pronunciation of `к` depends on the word's history, because loans keep velar [k] beside back vowels, so `q` cannot be placed without knowing the history. A runic back-series consonant asserts something else: agreement with the vowel beside it. Unicode's own account of the script classes the consonants as back-vowel, front-vowel, and neutral, not as uvular and velar [[U3]](#refU3). The vowel beside each consonant is written in the Cyrillic, in loans as in native words, so agreement can always be computed. *Космос* comes out as 𐰸𐰆𐰽𐰢𐰆𐰽, with the back-series `к` that agreement with `о` requires, and the spelling makes no claim that the speaker's `к` is uvular.

The cost a lexicon would have paid does not disappear. It moves from the writer to the reader. A reader trained on the inscriptions reads the back `к` as [q] and has to know, as a reader of Cyrillic does, that *космос* is a loan. That is the knowledge Kyrgyz Cyrillic already asks of every reader, since one `к` serves both sounds. The Latin case differs because Latin has a neutral `k`, which asks nothing of the writer. The runic script has no neutral `к`. No choice is free, and agreement is the only choice the source text can compute.

The same argument covers the cases that look like counterexamples. *Каракол* and *Каракөл*, the pair `WHITEPAPER.md` §8 uses to show what `q` would buy, are 𐰴𐰀𐰺𐰀𐰸𐰆𐰞 and 𐰴𐰀𐰺𐰀𐰜𐰇𐰠: they differ in their last syllable's `к` and `л` as well as in the vowel. They do so not because the converter knows they are different towns but because the vowels differ, and in this script the consonants follow the vowels.

## 11. `о` and `у`, and the Way Back

### 11.1 The merger

Unicode has one sign, 𐰆 `ORKHON O`, for `o` and `u`, and no Yenisei counterpart that could be pressed into service as the chart does for `ы`/`и` and `ө`/`ү`. No other character in the block can carry the distinction without abusing a sign with an unrelated value. A variation selector was considered and rejected: no variation sequences are defined for the script, so one would be an invisible character that fonts ignore and that search and comparison treat inconsistently. The two vowels therefore merge, in native vocabulary, which is the merger the native-priority rule of `WHITEPAPER.md` §18.1 exists to forbid. There it is always loanword distinctions that are allowed to be lost; here both members are native.

Two numbers describe the merger, and they point in different directions.

**It creates few homographs.** In the corpus, 28.9% of word tokens contain `о` or `у`, yet only 12 rune spellings are shared by two different words, covering 24 of 6,269 word types (0.4%) and 0.2% of tokens. Every one of them is an `о`/`у` pair: *тор/тур*, *он/ун*, *тоо/туу*, *ошол/ушул*, *кору/куру*, *баалуу/баалоо*, *коюлган/куюлган*, *жолдордо/жолдорду*, and spelling variants such as *тоннелдин/туннелдин*. A reader with context will almost never be unable to tell which word is meant.

**It is hard to undo without a word list.** Reverse conversion has to choose `о` or `у` at every 𐰆 from the runes alone. Kyrgyz rounding harmony helps less than it first appears. A 𐰆 followed in the next syllable by `а` or `ы` is almost always `у` (382 to 11 in the training split), because a low vowel after `о` would itself be `о`. But `о` after `а` is native in the contracted verbal nouns (*сактоо*, *шайлоо*, *каттоо*, from stems in `-а`) as well as in loans (*район*, *транспорт*, *Жапаров*), so no rule of that kind reaches certainty. The best purely positional rule found, a table of 36 contexts read off the runes (Appendix B), was fitted on the treebank's training split and evaluated on its test split, with these results:

### 11.2 What the proposal does about it

- **Reverse conversion is offered for the plene register only**, with the default vowel letters. The epigraphic register is one-way (§12).
- **Every guessed `о`/`у` is marked.** The testbed's inspector highlights each one and lets the reader flip it with a click, so that a reverse conversion ends in text the reader has confirmed rather than text the engine has guessed.
- **No word list in version 1.** The homograph figure shows that a list of attested Cyrillic forms would restore nearly every word, and the optional loan-restoration list of `WHITEPAPER.md` §18.2 is a precedent for a list on the reverse side only. But that list is defined never to affect native vocabulary, and this one would exist for nothing else. Adding it would change the system's principle, not just its data, so it is left to the maintainer (§20.3).

The merger is the mirror image of one Jany-Latyn already accepts. The plain form merges *кол* (hand) with *көл* (lake) as `kol` and keeps *кул* (slave) apart. The runes keep *кол* and *көл* apart, as 𐰸𐰆𐰞 and 𐰜𐰇𐰠, and merge *кол* with *кул*. Neither form is lossless, and they lose different things.

## 12. Two Registers: Plene and Epigraphic

Jany-Latyn defines a full form and a plain form together, the second one-way (`WHITEPAPER.md` §19). Orhon-Enesaí does the same with two registers that differ in purpose but share that structure:

| | Plene (default) | Epigraphic |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Vowels | All written | `а`/`э` only word-finally; others in the first syllable and word-finally (§15) |
| Long vowels | Doubled | Single |
| Ligatures | None | 𐰡 for лт/лд, 𐰧 or 𐰦 for нт/нд, 𐰩 or 𐰨 for нч |
| Reversible | Yes, except `о`/`у` | No |
| Shared spellings in the corpus | 0.4% of types | 5.6% of types, 8.9% of tokens |
| Purpose | Reading and writing modern Kyrgyz | Looking like the inscriptions |

The epigraphic register's collisions show why it has to be one-way: *шаар* (city) and *шар* (balloon), *менен* (with) and *менин* (my), *алдын* and *алтын* (gold), *датка* and *адатка* all share spellings. This is the plain form's trade made for the opposite reason. The plain form gives up distinctions to be typable anywhere; the epigraphic register gives them up to be authentic.

## 13. Sorting and Search

**Sorting.** Codepoint order is also the default collation order for the block, and it files signs in Unicode's Orkhon-and-Yenisei sequence, which means nothing to a Kyrgyz reader. The testbed should sort runes by the Jany-Latyn alphabet of what each rune stands for (`WHITEPAPER.md` §17). A list of rune spellings then sorts as the reader already knows it, with 𐰆 at `о` because it stands for both. The back and front forms of one letter differ at the second level, and Orkhon and Yenisei variants of one value at the third. Like Jany-Latyn's own `compare`, this has to be a rank table in the engine, because `Intl.Collator` takes no tailoring.

**Search.** A rune's search key is the fold key of the Jany-Latyn letter it stands for, with 𐰆 folded to `o`. A rune index therefore matches *кол* and *кул* alike, which is the merger of §11 showing up in search. Matching a Latin or Cyrillic query against rune text means folding `u` to `o` on the query side for that comparison only. Whether that belongs in the shared `foldKey` or only in rune search is an open question, parallel to the `q`→`k` question `WHITEPAPER.md` Appendix B leaves open (§20.3).

# Part IV — Specification

## 14. Forward Conversion

1. **Pivot.** Convert the Cyrillic input to the canonical Jany-Latyn full form with the engine, all options at their Jany-Latyn defaults. Lowercase the result; runes have no case.
2. **Words.** A word is a maximal run of Jany-Latyn letters (`a b ç d e f g h i í j k l m n ŋ o ö p r s ş t u ü v y z`, plus compose-mode `ä`). Hyphens, digits, punctuation, and anything else pass through unchanged, so each part of a hyphenated name is a word of its own.
3. **Vowels.** Map each vowel by the table in §3, or, under the historical vowel option, `a e ä` → 𐰀, `y i` → 𐰃, `o u` → 𐰆, `ö ü` → 𐰇.
4. **Governing vowel.** For each consonant at position *k*, let *n* be the next vowel and *p* the previous one. If *n* exists and either *n* = *k*+1 or there is no *p*, the governor is *n*. Otherwise, if *p* exists, it is *p*. Otherwise the word has no vowel and the consonant takes the back series.
5. **Series.** The governor's class gives the series: `a o u y` back; `e i ö ü ä` front.
6. **Consonants.** Map by the table in §3, using the series and, for `к`, `т`, and `ч`, the governor's identity: `к` with `о`/`у` → 𐰸, with `ы` → 𐰶, with `ө`/`ү` → 𐰜; `т` with `о`/`у` → 𐱇; `ч` with `и` → 𐰱. Under the option that switches the syllabic signs off, `к` is 𐰴/𐰚, `т` 𐱄/𐱅, and `ч` 𐰲.
7. **Register.** Under the epigraphic register, apply §15 to the word after steps 3–6, so that the series is always computed from the full spelling.
8. **Separator.** Under the two-dot option, replace each run of spaces between two rune words with a single U+205A.

## 15. The Epigraphic Register

The epigraphic register is a stylization modelled on the tendency the encoding proposal describes [[U3]](#refU3). It is not the spelling of any particular inscription, and it is one-way.

1. **Length** is unwritten: a doubled vowel is written once.
2. **`а` and `э`/`е`** are written only when word-final.
3. **Other vowels** are written in the first syllable and when word-final, and omitted elsewhere.
4. **Ligatures** replace adjacent pairs in the original spelling: `лт`, `лд` → 𐰡; `нт`, `нд` → 𐰧 (chart forms) or 𐰦 (Orkhon forms); `нч` → 𐰩 or 𐰨. A pair brought together only by omitting a vowel is not ligated.

*Кыргыз* becomes 𐰶𐰃𐰺𐰍𐰔, *Манас* 𐰢𐰣𐰽, and *Теңир* 𐱅𐰭𐰼, which differs from the inscriptions' 𐱅𐰭𐰼𐰃 *täŋri* [[W1]](#refW1) only by its final 𐰃, the vowel modern Kyrgyz has moved inside the word.

## 16. Reverse Conversion

Defined for the plene register with the chart's vowel letters:

1. Map each rune to its Jany-Latyn letter: both series of a consonant to one letter, Orkhon and Yenisei variants to one letter, the syllabic signs to `k`, `t`, or `ç`, and the ligatures to their pairs.
2. Resolve each 𐰆 by Appendix B. The second half of a long vowel copies the first. Mark every resolved 𐰆 as guessed.
3. Hand the result to the engine's existing Jany-Latyn → Cyrillic conversion, which applies its own rules for `e`, `íe`, `ía`, `ío`, and `íu` (`WHITEPAPER.md` Appendix A).
4. A consonant whose series disagrees with its governing vowel is not an error: it is how someone typing directly may mark a loan's velar `к`, for example. It converts to the same Cyrillic letter, and the inspector may flag it.

## 18. Display

- **Direction.** The output element is `dir="rtl"`. Rune text embedded in a left-to-right line, such as an inspector label, must be isolated with `<bdi>` or `unicode-bidi: isolate`. Without isolation, a number or bracket that follows the runes joins the right-to-left run and moves to the wrong side of the word: in Chromium, "Kyrgyz: 𐰶𐰃𐰺𐰍𐰃𐰕 (2026)" displays with "(2026)" to the left of the runes.
- **Fonts.** System support is uneven, so the testbed ships Noto Sans Old Turkic (SIL OFL 1.1) [[F1]](#refF1) as a web font. That font covers the 73 runes, space, no-break space, and the zero-width joiner, and nothing else. The word divider U+205A and ordinary punctuation have to come from the next font in the stack, and the stack has to include one that has U+205A. Windows ships an Old Turkic face [[W1]](#refW1); Apple and Android coverage has not been checked.
- **Line breaking.** Chromium breaks lines after U+205A, so text divided only by ⁚ still wraps. Other engines have not been checked (§20.4).
- **Copying.** Copied rune text is in logical order (the first letter of a word first in memory), which is correct. An optional "copy with isolates" may wrap it in U+2067 … U+2069 for pasting into left-to-right documents.
- **Vertical display.** A display mode imitating a stele, rows set top to bottom in vertical writing, is possible with CSS writing modes and is left for later.

## 19. Input

The testbed offers two ways in. In **live conversion**, the reader types Cyrillic, or Jany-Latyn, on any keyboard and the runes appear as they type; the converter decides every series. In **direct entry**, an on-screen rune keyboard and operating-system layouts for macOS and Linux put each sign on a key.

The direct layout uses the physical positions of the Kyrgyz standard Cyrillic layout (`xkeyboard-config`, `symbols/kg`) [[K1]](#refK1), so a Kyrgyz typist finds each rune under the Cyrillic letter it stands for. Runes have no case, which frees Shift to carry the front series: `к` gives 𐰴, Shift+`к` gives 𐰚. On the vowel keys, Shift gives the vowel-bound sign the chart pairs with that vowel: Shift+`ы` 𐰶, Shift+`о` 𐰸, Shift+`ө` 𐰜, Shift+`у` 𐱇, Shift+`и` 𐰱. AltGr (Option on macOS) carries the ligatures and the remaining Yenisei variants, and `ц`, unused because Jany-Latyn writes it `ts`, carries the word divider ⁚. Every one of the 73 characters is reachable, and every rune the two plene presets emit is on the base or Shift layer. The full table is in the implementation brief.

# Part V — Open Questions

## 20. Open Questions and Tests

Each item states the claim, the test that would settle it, and the result that would count against it, as `WHITEPAPER.md` §23 does.

### 20.1 Legibility of the plene register

**Claim:** readers who know the chart read plene output more easily than epigraphic output, and Cyrillic-literate readers new to the script learn it faster from plene output.
**Test:** timed reading of matched passages in both registers by readers who learned the chart, and a short learning study for new readers.
**What would refute it:** no difference, or a preference for the epigraphic register among readers who know the chart, which would argue for making it the default for display use.

### 20.2 The chart as a convention

**Claim:** the chart's assignments, particularly for `ж`, the vowel variants, and the loan consonants, are shared by other Kyrgyz rune charts in circulation, and are not one author's.
**Test:** collect the charts in use in Kyrgyz education, heritage, and social media, and tabulate their assignments against Appendix A.
**What would refute it:** wide disagreement on any of those points, which would argue for the Unicode-strict preset as the default (§20.6), or for an option per competing convention.

### 20.3 `о`/`у` and the reverse conversion

**Claim:** a positional rule restores enough of the text that, with every guess marked and flippable, the reverse conversion is useful without a word list.
**Test:** report the held-out figures of §11 on a second corpus of a different genre, and measure how many clicks a reader needs per hundred words to finish a reverse conversion.
**What would refute it:** materially lower accuracy on a second corpus, or a correction burden readers will not accept. Either would put the word-list question of §11.2 to the maintainer. The parallel question of whether `foldKey` should fold `u` to `o` for rune search is open in the same way.

### 20.4 Fonts and platforms

**Claim:** with the web font bundled, the mode renders correctly in current browsers on desktop and mobile.
**Test:** render the specimen in Chromium, Firefox, and Safari on desktop, iOS, and Android, and check glyph coverage, right-to-left order, line breaking after U+205A, and the fallback for U+205A.
**What would refute it:** a mainstream engine that misorders the runs or will not break after the divider, which would require inserting U+200B after each divider in the output.

### 20.5 Tokenization

**Claim (prediction):** language-model tokenizers spend several tokens per rune, since the script is rare in training data and each rune is four UTF-8 bytes, so runic text costs several times as many tokens as Cyrillic.
**Test:** the tokenization test of `WHITEPAPER.md` §23.1, with runes added as a configuration.
**What would refute it:** nothing in this mode depends on the result; it is recorded so that no one mistakes the runes for an encoding meant for machines.

### 20.6 Which preset should be the default

The chart and the Unicode-strict preset differ on five signs (§7.3). This draft defaults to the chart, because readers are being shown the chart. The case for the strict preset is that it uses every character as Unicode defines it, so text produced in the testbed would mean the same thing to every other tool. The question is for the maintainer, and §20.2 bears on it.
